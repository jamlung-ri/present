Build the Network envelope version

One round, for tables of six, seven or eight. Every table opens an envelope and tries to build the same network. What nobody is told is that the envelopes are different, and only one of them has everything a table needs.

When time is up, each table that fell short tells the room what it did not have. The one table that finished goes last and explains what went right. Between them, the tables name the ingredients of a working terminology programme.

How it plays

Open the envelope

Each person takes a role card and its chain cards. Role cards are face up and tables may talk, unless their envelope says otherwise.

Build one chain

Cards connect only when the touching ends match, and cards can be turned round. The table wins by laying one chain that uses every card and keeps every role's rule. Six minutes.

Tell the room

Tables that fell short say what they did not have. The table that finished goes last and says what went right.

The secret in this version sits with the table and not the player. Everyone can see the roles at their own table. Nobody knows that the next table was dealt something different.

The roles in a complete envelope

Six roles are the core, and every one holds cards and has one rule. A seventh and eighth player join as extra Champions: owners of systems that already use the shared symbols and are ready to connect. Roles that are not in play at the chosen size are faded.

What the room ends up with

As the tables report, the facilitator writes one line for each on a flip chart. By the time the winning table speaks, the list is already on the wall, and that table had all of it.

  • A map between code systems. Systems that use different codes do not connect by themselves.
  • Someone keeping the map current. A map that is out of date connects nothing.
  • A forum where people can ask. A map nobody can ask for is as good as no map.
  • Every system at the table. A missing stakeholder leaves a gap nobody else can fill.
  • One agreed standard. Two standards that each expect to be adopted cannot both lead.

The closing question for the room: which envelope is your country holding today? Nobody has to answer aloud. It is the question the two days are built to help with.

Practical points

  • Hand out envelopes at random and mix countries at each table, so no delegation is the one that "failed" or "won".
  • Tables of different sizes can play side by side. Every envelope exists in a six, seven and eight-person form, and the same thing is missing at each size.
  • The empty-chair envelope seats one fewer, so give it to the smallest table.
  • With more than six tables, repeat one of the incomplete envelopes. With fewer, drop one. Keep exactly one complete envelope.
  • Say clearly at the reveal that no table failed through its own doing.

Still to settle

  • Whether six minutes is enough for the complete table to finish. If it runs out of time, the report still works: ask what it had that others did not.
  • Whether a rigged game suits a room of executives. The reveal has to land as a shared joke.
  • Whether funding should be one of the envelopes. This version leaves it out.
  • One playtest of the complete envelope and one of an incomplete one.
