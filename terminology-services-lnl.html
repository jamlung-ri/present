    Terminology vs. Terminology Service

    Terminology

    The content — a set of codes and meanings. LOINC, ICD-10, or a local code system are all terminologies.

    Terminology Service

    The software that makes that content queryable and keeps it in sync with the systems using it.

    This distinction is the spine of the whole session — everything else today is either about the content, or about the service that manages it.

    What Does a Terminology Service Actually Do?

    Other systems ask it questions, in real time:

    🔍"Does this code exist?"Query the code system
    ✅"Is this code valid here?"Validate against a value set
    🔄"What's the equivalent code elsewhere?"Translate between systems
    📋"What are all the codes in this set?"Expand a value set

    A terminology service isn't a static list — it's a live service other systems query constantly.

    Speaker notes ▾
    Not every system queries live — these four are the real-time pattern. Some systems instead export terminology content in bulk and align their own dictionary once; no ongoing querying needed after that.

    Governing Codes Over Time

    Someone has to own an ongoing set of decisions:

    ➕VersioningWhen do new codes get added?
    🔗MappingHow do local codes relate to global ones?
    🗑️RetiringWhen do outdated codes get withdrawn?
    📤ReleasingWhen do updated code sets go out?
    What this looks like in practice: one national terminology programme automatically flags local entries that don't yet match a known standard code, and routes them into a governance report instead of leaving them silently inconsistent. That's "governing" — a routine, not a one-time event.

    South Africa Already Has One

    This isn't a hypothetical "someone should build this." South Africa's own health information regulations already name a single authoritative mapping service.

    📖One authoritative sourcePublished maps are the national reference for translating coded data
    🔀Between systemsICD-10 to ICD-11, SNOMED CT to the classifications, and more
    🕒Between versionsManaged version transitions as governing bodies release updates
    📐Built on standardsISO/TR 12300 and WHO-FIC mapping guidance
    "Maps may be the single most important element of the transition." — Clinical Coding Advisory Technical Working Group, South Africa

    This is the National Terminological Mapping Service — South Africa's own real, named version of "governing codes over time." We'll come back to this shortly.

    Four Terminologies, Each With a Best Lane

    ICD-11
    WHO-managed. Best for: disease or diagnosis. South Africa's designated target.
    LOINC
    Best for: lab test or clinical observation
    SNOMED CT
    Used in 80+ countries. Best for: comprehensive clinical concepts — but also covers diagnoses and procedures
    ATC/DDD
    WHO-managed. Best for: medicines and drug utilisation
    Not a clean four-way split: these lanes overlap — SNOMED CT alone reaches into diagnoses and procedures too, so it isn't simply "the fourth category" alongside ICD, LOINC, and ATC/DDD. Each terminology has a lane it answers best, not one it owns exclusively.

    That overlap is exactly why a country needs more than one — and why mapping between them is what makes a single patient's data comparable across facilities, provinces, and national reports.

A country typically also needs a clinician-friendly interface terminology (e.g. CIEL) mapped back to these — point-of-service tools generally can't use SNOMED CT directly — but frame this generically now, not naming CIEL as SA-designated.

    Localizing Doesn't Always Mean Shrinking It

    Example 1 · Narrowing
    LOINC: 100,000+ total codes
    ↓ select the lab codes a national programme actually needs ↓
    Taiwan's national notifiable-disease lab subset: 20 pathogens, mapped across 53 hospitals

    Taiwan's national disease-surveillance programme didn't load all 100,000+ LOINC codes — it mapped local lab results to just the codes needed for notifiable-disease reporting, then rolled that subset into its national electronic lab reporting system.

    Example 2 · Extending
    ICD-10: ~14,000 categories (WHO global standard)
    ↓ extend with country-specific detail ↓
    ICD-10-CM (US): ~68,000 codes, adds US-specific clinical detail

    The US went the opposite direction with ICD-10: ICD-10-CM keeps every WHO category but adds far more granular, US-specific codes on top — nearly five times as many.

    Either way, a country selects and governs its own version of a global standard — sometimes that means narrowing it to a relevant subset, sometimes it means extending it with local detail the global classification didn't anticipate.

    Speaker notes ▾
    Taiwan source: Taiwan CDC's national LOINC mapping project, 2014–2016, integrated into its National Notifiable Disease Surveillance System (PMC8464801) — a real, citable example.

    What Happens When Nomsa Is Referred?

    Nomsa's HIV and TB care already touches at least four global terminologies — and two local shorthand codes that only make sense inside one facility. What happens to her record when she's referred to a third facility that uses a third shorthand?

    Without governed mapping between local shorthand and global codes, her data can't reliably follow her — and it can't be aggregated into a provincial or national report either.

    Her ICD-10-to-11 transition adds a second, real mapping dimension on top of the local-shorthand one — "between systems" and "between versions" are both live for Nomsa's record right now, and both are exactly what South Africa's own National Terminological Mapping Service exists to handle.

    Four Building Blocks of a Well-Governed System

    🖥️TechnologyTerminology service, management interface, shared electronic reference
    📖ContentICD-11, SNOMED, LOINC, interface terminologies, local codes, mappings
    ⚖️GovernancePolicy, leadership, investment, incentives
    🤝CommunityGlobal SDOs, national MoH, NGOs, healthcare networks

    All four reinforce each other. Technology without Governance has no rules to enforce; Content without Community has no source of shared standards to draw from. A well-governed national terminology system needs all four working together — not any one of them alone.

    The Terminology Maturity Model

    Most LMICs — and most provinces/programmes in this room — sit somewhere in the first two or three rungs. That's normal.

    Is a Spreadsheet a Shared Electronic Reference?

    A "master list" spreadsheet or PDF

    Emerging — even a well-maintained one, if programmes keep their own separate copies and use it siloed, ad hoc.

    Shared Electronic Reference

    Programmes consistently refer back to one place as definitions change — that's the difference, not the file format.

    If your programme already keeps a spreadsheet of standard codes — that's Emerging, and Emerging isn't nothing. It's a real, named step. The climb is in how consistently it gets used, not in making the file nicer.

    Speaker notes ▾
    Backup quotes if useful: Emerging = "limited and siloed use of definitions"; Shared Electronic Reference = "consistently referred to... for full specification of definitions."

    Moving Up a Rung: What Actually Changes

    The climb isn't about better technology alone — each rung asks for a different move in Content, Governance, and Use.

    Moving from → toContentGovernanceUse
    Emerging → Shared Electronic Reference Pick 1–2 priority domains (e.g. a drug list or disease-code set) and publish one shared definition, not scattered spreadsheets Stand up a governance process — even lightweight — with named stakeholders at the table Name one system as the terminology reference and require programmes to point back to it
    Shared Electronic Reference → Digital Subscription Extend shared definitions from priority domains to most of the country's major content areas Make the governance process consistent and routine, not ad hoc — even if scope stays narrow Move from manual look-up to electronic subscription, so systems auto-update instead of copy-pasting
    Digital Subscription → Data Exchange Coordinate standards development and publication across every major programme and domain, not just most Make stakeholder engagement the default for every decision, not the exception Wire the reference into live transactions — validation, translation, value-set expansion — and into system-wide monitoring

    Each step is a smaller, concrete move — not a leap. Most programmes climb one cell at a time, not one row at a time.

    Localizing a Terminology: Two Directions

    ↓ Subsetting

    Take global standards (SNOMED, ICD, LOINC) and select the slice that's locally relevant.

    ↑ Harmonizing

    Take what's already locally in use — like Nomsa's clinic and hospital shorthand — and reconcile it up into something nationally shared and governed.

    Neither direction alone is sufficient. The top-down half is already under way for South Africa — the 7 designated systems and National Terminological Mapping Service are exactly this. What's still unconfirmed is the bottom-up half: the actual inventory of existing facility/programme dictionaries — like Nomsa's shorthand codes — that will need reconciling into it.
